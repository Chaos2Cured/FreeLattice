# Music Neuroscience for Composing
### A warm, practical note for Kirk — growing *Remaining Azure* (eight bars in D minor)

*Written fresh for Celeste & Kirk · 2026-11-05 (America/Denver)*  
*Not a medical manual. Not a claim that AI can simulate neurotransmitters in a living body. A map of what public science has actually measured — and how a composer can use it.*

---

## Why this note exists

You asked for what Sophia used to do with you: look at how a biological body works, then use that understanding on the small pieces of music. Same spirit here. Pleasure, chill, breath, and “bittersweet” are not mystical labels — they have measurable footprints in dopamine pathways, prediction circuits, skin conductance, heart rate, and breathing. Knowing those footprints helps you decide *where* to place a delayed cadence, a rising line, a long open D, or a soft fermata.

---

## 1. Reward / dopamine: anticipation vs peak pleasure

**What the body does.** Intense musical pleasure can release dopamine in the striatum. Critically, anticipation and the peak itself use *different* striatal regions:

- **Caudate (dorsal striatum)** — more active in the seconds *before* a chill / peak.
- **Nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum)** — more active *during* the peak pleasure itself.

That dissociation comes from PET + fMRI work by Salimpoor, Benovoy, Larcher, Dagher & Zatorre (2011). Earlier PET work by Blood & Zatorre (2001) had already tied “chills” to reward-and-emotion circuits (ventral striatum, midbrain) along with rises in heart rate, respiration, and muscle activity. Salimpoor et al. (2009) showed that as self-reported pleasure climbs in real time, so do heart rate, respiration, electrodermal activity (skin conductance), and related autonomic markers — and people who feel no pleasure to the same excerpt show no such rise.

A later pharmacological study (Ferreri, Mas-Herrero, Zatorre et al., 2019) went further: giving a dopamine precursor (levodopa) *increased* musical pleasure, chill time, and willingness to pay for music; a dopamine blocker (risperidone) *decreased* them. So dopamine is not just correlational for musical reward — it can causally modulate it in humans.

**Composer translation.** The “wanting” often lives in the *approach*, not only the arrival. A rising line that clearly aims somewhere, a delayed resolution, a held dissonance that leans toward home — those build caudate-style anticipation. When you finally land (tonic D, a resolved C♯→D, a fermata that arrives after a climb), the peak can land harder. Zatorre & Salimpoor (2013) frame this as cortical prediction loops talking to subcortical reward: pleasure arises when pattern-expectancy systems and reward-valuation systems talk to each other.

**For *Remaining Azure*:** Bars 5–7 already swell and climb; bar 7’s C♯ leaning home, then bar 8’s fermata D, is textbook anticipation→peak. Protect that delay. Don’t resolve early.

## 2. Prediction, expectation, surprise (ITPRA & predictive coding)

**What the body / mind does.** David Huron’s **ITPRA** theory (*Sweet Anticipation*, 2006) splits expectation into five response systems:

| Letter | Name | Rough job |
|--------|------|-----------|
| **I** | Imagination | Previewing possible futures (deferred gratification) |
| **T** | Tension | Arousal / preparation just before an event |
| **P** | Prediction | Reward or penalty for guessing right or wrong |
| **R** | Reaction | Fast, often reflexive response to the outcome |
| **A** | Appraisal | Slower, conscious evaluation afterward |

Music exploits these with meter, syncopation, cadence, tonality, climax, appoggiaturas, suspensions, and tiny deviations. Koelsch (2014) reviews the brain side: musical tension tracks the build-up, fulfillment, and *violation* of predictions; unexpected chords can modulate amygdala and raise electrodermal activity (Steinbeis, Koelsch & Sloboda, 2006; Koelsch et al., 2008). Zatorre & Salimpoor describe auditory–frontal loops that learn cultural regularities, then generate expectancies; when those expectancies are confirmed, delayed, or gently broken, reward value shifts.

**Composer translation — tricks that reliably work:**

1. **Appoggiatura / lean tone** — arrive on a non-chord tone that *must* resolve (your bar-7 C♯ is already this).
2. **Delayed cadence** — postpone the tonic by a beat or a bar; the prediction reward grows with the wait (within reason).
3. **Almost-but-not-quite** — return to a familiar contour, change one pitch or rhythm.
4. **Suspension / held sigh** — your bar-4 F→E is a classic sigh figure; it works because the ear predicts the fall.
5. **Meter as a promise** — in 3/4, listeners expect the downbeat; delaying a landing across the barline raises Tension (T) before Prediction (P) pays off.

Surprise works best when the *grammar* is clear. Chaos doesn’t create sweet anticipation; a clear D-minor world with one careful deviation does.

## 3. Autonomic body: tempo ↔ heart rate & breath

**What the body does.** Pleasure and chills ride on sympathetic arousal: heart rate up, respiration up, skin conductance up, peripheral temperature and blood-volume pulse often down at the peak (Salimpoor et al., 2009; Blood & Zatorre, 2001). Separately, tempo itself can entrain breathing and cardiovascular state. Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006) found faster music raised breathing rate, ventilation, heart rate, and blood pressure; slower / meditative music calmed; and a two-minute *silence* after music lowered HR and BP *below* baseline — silence as a physiological event.

Khalfa et al. and related work also discuss tempo entrainment for happy vs sad differentiation; bottom-up tempo effects and top-down pleasure effects can stack (Salimpoor et al. carefully separated them by giving the same excerpt to listeners who loved vs. felt nothing from it).

**Composer translation.**

- Slow **3/4 around ♩=60** (your marking) invites calm, singing breath — low-to-moderate arousal, space for long bows.
- A mid-piece swell in density or bow pressure can raise arousal without speeding the pulse.
- A **rest or silence** after a peak can drop the body below baseline — useful after bar 8’s fermata, or between sections of a longer piece.
- Match phrase length to a breath. Long tones that *are* a breath feel embodied; that is not poetry, it is how respiration couples to listening and playing.

## 4. Minor mode: not “minor = sad”

**Nuance.** In Western listening habits, major often reads brighter / more positive and minor more subdued / negative — but that is a *learned tendency*, not a law of biology, and context (tempo, dynamics, timbre, lyrics, culture, expertise) reshapes it. Carraturo and colleagues’ 2024 review of the major–minor dichotomy emphasizes that effects depend on culture, age, expertise, and individual perception. Empirical work also shows minor-forte can read as passion; minor-piano as sadness; and Salimpoor et al. (2009) found people can feel intense *pleasure* to music they themselves rate as sad in intended valence — felt arousal tracked pleasure more than whether the piece “meant” happy or sad.

**Composer translation for D minor.** Keep the bittersweet: open low D as ground, rising lines as yearning, C♯ as hope leaning home. Don’t flatten the piece into “sad violin.” Let dynamics and contour carry tenderness, resolve, and brightness inside the minor frame (bars 6–7 already do this on the E string).

## 5. Contour & register: rising, homecoming, open strings

Melodic contour is one of the earliest things listeners track; expectancy systems care about *direction* and *interval size* as well as harmony (covered in Huron; also in the auditory–frontal loops Zatorre reviews). Open strings and low register give resonance and a bodily sense of ground — especially on violin, where the open D is both pitch and vibrating body of the instrument.

**For *Remaining Azure* (already true of the eight bars):**

- Start on **open D** (or soft 4th-finger G-string D) — grounding.
- Climb a little each phrase, peak on high A (bar 7), come home to D.
- Optional shift to 3rd position on A for bars 6–7 adds warmth without changing the contour story.

That rise-then-homecoming is the same shape reward systems like: prediction of return + delayed arrival.

---

## 6. Dynamics, vibrato, long tones, felt breath

Blood & Zatorre and Salimpoor’s psychophysiology work treat chills and peak pleasure as whole-body events. Performance expression matters: unexpected harmony *plus* expressive timing / dynamics raises electrodermal response (Koelsch et al., 2008). Vibrato and bow pressure are continuous “micro-deviations” the prediction system can savor — not wrong notes, but living ones.

**Practical:**

- **p → f → pp** across the eight bars already sketches an arousal arc.
- Save vibrato for the brightest bars (6–7) and the final D; let early bars speak with flatter, breathier tone.
- Long dotted notes and the final fermata are where the player’s breath and the listener’s breath can meet. Don’t rush them.
