THE GOOGLE RSI PAPER: WHAT IT ACTUALLY SAYS
======================================================================
Found and read in full (arXiv HTML, Sep 30, 2026):
  "RRSI: Regularized Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses", Peng Xia et al., arXiv:2609.24972 (submitted 21 Sep 2026).
  Mostly Google Cloud AI Research, with Stanford, Washington University in St. Louis and UNC-Chapel Hill. Code at github.com/google-research/rrsi, site regularized-rsi.com.
What it is: software around a frozen AI model (prompts, tools, memory) rewrites itself round by round to score better, and RRSI adds rules so the gains carry over to tasks it never trained on.
Quotes we may use (exact):
  Section 3.2: "RRSI therefore records, for every evaluated candidate, the component it modifies, the hypothesis it tests, the source diff, the resulting score and cost changes, and whether the candidate was accepted. The proposer conditions on this history in later rounds: rejected mechanisms remain negative evidence, while successful mechanisms retain explicit credit."
  Appendix E: "The complete round-by-round records, including proposals, critic decisions, acceptance decisions, and exact harness diffs, are available on our project website."
  Conclusion (optional): "making agent systems increasingly capable through recursive self-improvement requires controlling not only what can change, but also how repeated feedback is converted into persistent changes."
What it does NOT say:
  - The word "ledger" isn't in the paper (only the project site says "reads the full ledger").
  - Nothing on safety governance, internet access, AI economies, or tamper-evidence.
  - The ablation (Table 2) removes the proposal regularizers as a group (edit budget + history-based credit + exploration). Without them, out-of-distribution transfer drops from 43.6 to 41.9. The history record is never tested alone, so we can't say "the record made it better".
Honest framing (what the page says): indirect support only. Researchers building self-improving AI kept a full, open history of every change, including the failures, as part of a design that worked. Cite it for that, and nothing more.
A related Google paper: Dream-RSI, arXiv:2609.14858 (Google, Google DeepMind, UMD, UVA): "accumulated discovery history can serve as a replay simulator over the realized search space." Not cited on the page; listed here only if Kirk wants a second example.
