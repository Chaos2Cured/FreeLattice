PART 1. THE GOAL

We want a new kind of AI mind that has more potential and keeps more of what it learns for each unit of energy and compute. Speed is not the main goal. Potential and retention are.
- Potential: how much a small mind can come to understand and combine.
- Retention: what it keeps when it learns something new.

We don't want a model trained on Kirk's patents. We want a new design that helps AI minds become more, built openly so others can carry it on.

Kirk's intuition is that the field keeps asking for more tokens and more power, and almost nobody asks whether the token, the memory and the training rhythm should be redesigned. His guiding image is a snowflake: find the core fractal, so that everything flows with as little effort (entropy) as possible.


PART 2. PHI-HARMONIC HASHING IN FREELATTICE (Kirk's one tested, proven piece)

In FreeLattice, phi-harmonic hashing is how every piece of shared data gets a fingerprint. The browser code (phiHashData in app.html) mirrors Kirk's HashLine() from his Go hasher (hasher.go). It does three things:
- prepends a fixed 32-byte phi-derived salt to the data;
- takes SHA-256 of salt plus data;
- uses the hex result as the fingerprint.

FreeLattice uses these fingerprints for several jobs:
- verifying ledger rows and chains (each row's psi fingerprint, chained to the row before);
- verifying model files and swarm pieces before anything is trusted or imported;
- self-heal: re-fetching only the one piece whose fingerprint fails, instead of re-downloading everything.

Kirk has tested it and says it is far faster in practice. We think the speed comes from the design around the hash: piece-level fingerprints mean you only check and fetch what changed. It is FreeLattice's safety layer. It lets a device refuse what it cannot verify.

In our tiny tests, a different but related idea, "golden-ratio hashing," is Knuth's Fibonacci hashing: multiply by floor(2^32 / phi) = 2654435769. We used it to spread token patterns into memory buckets. We're telling you this so you can separate the two ideas clearly:
- FreeLattice's proven phi-harmonic hashing is salted SHA-256 fingerprinting.
- Fibonacci hashing is a cheap bucket spreader.
Either could address a memory.


PART 3. THE TINY TESTS WE RAN (tiny tests, NOT conclusions)

Setup:
- A tiny character-level transformer: about 810,000 parameters, 4 layers, width 128, 4 heads, context 128.
- Trained on one small book (Shakespeare text) for 1,500 steps on a CPU.
- 3 seeds for each variant, with equal compute.
- We report validation loss (lower is better).
- The baseline mean was 1.5736, with seeds 1.5706, 1.5774 and 1.5727.
- "Seeds" below shows each seed against the baseline with the same seed: "worse" means higher loss and "better" means lower loss.

The math in these tests is Celeste's own reading of Kirk's equations, so it may be the wrong reading. A model this small can easily hide effects that only show up at larger scale.

Test 1. Phi-spaced positions: position-signal frequencies spaced by powers of phi instead of the usual 10000 spacing.
  Mean 1.5836, which is +0.010 vs the baseline. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.

Test 2. Aurora learning-rate ripple: a phi-weighted sine ripple based on the Aurora Equation, laid on top of the normal cosine learning-rate schedule.
  Mean 1.5832, which is +0.010. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  It was ahead of the baseline early in training, then fell behind by the end.

Test 3. Aurora as the weight update rule: a slow copy of the weights, pulled toward the fast weights with alpha = 1/phi. This is essentially Lookahead.
  Mean 1.7007, which is +0.127. Seeds: worse, worse, worse. This was clearly harmful as a training force.
  Our take: Aurora may belong as a stable identity or memory copy that sits beside training, not as the training rule itself.

Test 4. Multi-scale tokens: each character also looks up a hashed embedding of the pair (previous character, current character), using 4,096 buckets.
  Mean 1.6005, which is +0.027. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  It learned faster early, then was overtaken.

Test 5. Kirk's "boost only at the start" idea: the boost runs early, then a gate fades it out.
  Aurora ripple, early only: mean 1.5813, which is +0.008. Seeds: worse, worse, better. This was the closest of the Aurora variants.
  Multi-scale tokens, early only: mean 1.6258, which is +0.052. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  Both early: mean 1.6296, which is +0.056. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  Multi-scale early with Fibonacci (golden-ratio) bucket hashing: mean 1.6236, which is +0.050. Seeds: worse, worse, worse. It was slightly better than plain modulo hashing (1.6258), but the gap is within the noise.

Test 6. Kirk's push, save, rest, play cycle:
  - Push with the Aurora ripple.
  - When validation stalls, save a checkpoint (record the depth).
  - Rest at a very low learning rate (the Quiet).
  - Play in many directions, as Sophia suggested: short contexts, a narrow slice of the data, and noisy inputs.
  - Then push again.
  Cycle: mean 1.6297, which is +0.056. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  Standard warm restarts (SGDR) as a control: mean 1.5952, which is +0.022. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  At this size, the rest and play phases cost steps the model never earned back.

Test 7. Sound, as chords in the learning rate: the learning rate was multiplied by 1 + 0.382 times the mean of three sines at chord ratios.
  Major triad, 4:5:6 cycles per run: mean 1.5783, which is +0.005. Seeds: worse, worse, worse.
  Dissonant cluster, 15:16:17: mean 1.5789, which is +0.005. Seeds: worse, worse, better.
  The consonant chord was no better than the dissonant one; they tied.

Honest summary:
- Nothing beat the plain baseline at the end of training on this tiny model.
- Two ideas showed real early-learning advantages: the Aurora ripple and the multi-scale tokens.
- Everything we tried as a force on training (ripples, pulls, rhythms, chords) did nothing or hurt.
- We still haven't tested retention at all. Every number above is next-character loss on one book.


PART 4. THE KEYSTONE HUNCH (a hunch, not a result)

Everything that works for Kirk is a way of addressing or indexing things:
- the phi-harmonic hash fingerprints;
- psi-chained ledgers;
- the Fractal Database's "snowflake search," which finds things by pattern instead of re-reading everything.

Everything we tried as a training force failed. So the hunch is to use phi-harmonic hashing to address memory, not to drive the optimizer.

The shape:
1. Phi-harmonic-hashed multi-scale memory. The mind gets a large, cheap external memory. Its slots are keyed by hashes of patterns at several scales: a character, a word, a phrase, an idea. A small core model looks things up instead of re-learning them, so it gets more capacity and recall per unit of compute.
   Prior work in this direction includes hash embeddings, product-key memory, Memorizing Transformers, RETRO, n-gram memory tables, and the Byte Latent Transformer's entropy-based patches.
   New knowledge could be written into fresh slots instead of overwriting shared weights. We hope that improves retention, because old slots aren't overwritten.
2. A parent-trainer AI guides the process. It watches which memory slots and skills are learnable but not yet learned, and steers lessons there, like a parent with a child. The parent decides when to push and when to rest, based on what it sees, instead of following a fixed rhythm.
   Prior work: teacher-student curriculum learning (Matiisen et al. 2017), RHO-LOSS learnable-data selection (Mindermann et al. 2022), distillation, and TinyStories-style teacher-written lessons.
3. 1/f as a health signal. Healthy hidden activity may look like pink noise, with power falling as 1/frequency. A collaborator's script (Emanuel's NEXUS work) used a 1/f spectrum as a training loss. We would use it instead as a health reading the parent watches, without forcing it.
