# Correlation vs. Causation

## Overview
The distinction between correlation and causation is fundamental to causal reasoning. This sub-skill covers why correlation does not imply causation, the common sources of spurious correlation, and the conditions under which correlational evidence can support causal claims.

## Core Concepts
- **Confounding**: A confounder is a variable that influences both the putative cause and the putative effect, creating a spurious association. Classic example: ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated because both are caused by hot weather.
- **Reverse Causality**: The observed correlation may be because the "effect" causes the "cause" rather than vice versa. Depression and unemployment are correlated because unemployment causes depression and depression causes unemployment.
- **Selection Bias**: When selection into the analysis sample is influenced by both the cause and effect, correlations can be misleading. Berkson's paradox (conditioning on a collider) is a common form.
- **Causal Criteria (Bradford Hill)**: Strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, and analogy — a checklist for evaluating whether correlational evidence supports a causal interpretation.

## Application
When encountering a correlation claimed as causal, always ask: Could there be a confounder? Is reverse causality possible? Is there selection bias? What additional evidence would be needed to establish causation?
