# Moral and Ethical Reasoning

## Overview

Moral and ethical reasoning provides the frameworks and protocols for systematically analyzing questions of right and wrong, good and bad, duty and consequence. This skill covers the major normative ethical traditions (deontology, consequentialism, virtue ethics, care ethics, social contract theory), the psychological processes underlying moral judgment, and the application of ethical reasoning to real-world domains including technology, medicine, business, and policy. The goal is not to produce simple answers to moral questions but to structure deliberation so that ethical choices are made deliberately, transparently, and defensibly.

## Core Principles

- **Principle 1  Multi-Framework Analysis:** No single ethical theory captures all morally relevant considerations. Evaluate decisions from at least two frameworks (e.g., deontological rules + consequentialist outcomes) to reveal blind spots and trade-offs. Where frameworks converge, confidence increases; where they conflict, the tension itself is the most important finding.

- **Principle 2  Distinguish Evaluated Action from Evaluation Framework:** A common error is conflating the moral analysis (the tool) with the action being analyzed (the target). Be explicit about which framework is being applied and why. The same action may be required by one framework and forbidden by another; this is not a contradiction but a feature of genuine moral complexity.

- **Principle 3  Stakeholder-Centric Analysis:** Ethical decisions affect multiple parties with potentially competing interests. A complete ethical analysis identifies all affected stakeholders, articulates how each is impacted, and weighs these impacts systematically. Missing stakeholders is the most common source of ethical failure.

## Components

### 1. Deontology (Duty Ethics)
The morality of an action is based on whether the action itself is right or wrong under a set of rules, duties, or principles, regardless of consequences.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Kantian Ethics:** Categorical imperative (act only according to that maxim by which you can will that it become universal law); Formula of Humanity (treat persons always as ends, never merely as means)
- **Rule-Based Ethics:** W.D. Ross's prima facie duties (fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence); duty-based constraints on action independent of consequences
- **Rights-Based Ethics:** Negative rights (rights to non-interference) vs. positive rights (rights to provision); inalienable vs. contingent rights; right to privacy, autonomy, informed consent
- **Key Applications:** Human subjects research (IRB protocols, informed consent, Belmont Report), legal ethics (attorney-client privilege, zealous representation boundaries), professional codes of conduct

### 2. Consequentialism (Outcome-Based Ethics)
The morality of an action is judged entirely by its consequences.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Utilitarianism:** Maximize overall utility (happiness, well-being, preference satisfaction); Act utilitarianism (evaluate each action individually) vs. Rule utilitarianism (evaluate the general rule)
- **Prioritarian & Egalitarian Variants:** Weight benefits to the worst-off more heavily (prioritarianism); equality as intrinsic good (egalitarianism); sufficientarianism (ensure everyone meets a threshold)
- **Expected Utility Frameworks:** Decision theory under uncertainty for ethical choices; expected value of actions considering probabilities and magnitudes of outcomes
- **Key Applications:** Triage and resource allocation (medical, disaster response), public policy cost-benefit analysis, effective altruism (charity evaluation, cause prioritization)

### 3. Virtue Ethics
The morality of an action is determined by the character of the agentwhat a virtuous person would do.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Aristotelian Virtues:** The Golden Mean (courage between cowardice and recklessness; generosity between stinginess and profligacy); eudaimonia (flourishing as the ultimate goal)
- **Modern Virtue Frameworks:** MacIntyre's practice-based virtues (goods internal to practices); Nussbaum's capabilities approach; Swanton's pluralistic virtue ethics
- **Key Virtues for AI & Technology:** Prudence (technological caution, foresight), integrity (consistency between values and actions), intellectual humility (acknowledging limits and uncertainties), accountability (willingness to justify and bear consequences)
- **Applications:** Professional ethics education, moral character assessment, technology ethics (what virtues should AI systems embody?)

### 4. Care Ethics
The morality of an action is grounded in relationships, care, and responsibility to others.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Relational Moral Agency:** Moral agents are embedded in networks of relationships; ethical decisions must consider the caring bonds and mutual dependencies
- **Contextual Particularity:** Universal principles are less important than attending to the specific needs, relationships, and circumstances of those involved
- **Key Concepts:** Attentiveness (noticing need), responsibility (acting on need), competence (effective care), responsiveness (the cared-for's perspective)
- **Applications:** Healthcare ethics (patient-centered care, nursing ethics), family and community decision-making, social policy affecting vulnerable populations

### 5. Social Contract Ethics
The morality of an action is determined by the implicit or explicit agreements that rational individuals would accept.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Classical Social Contract:** Hobbes (self-interest ? sovereign), Locke (natural rights ? limited government), Rousseau (general will ? collective self-governance)
- **Modern Contractarianism:** Rawls's veil of ignorance (principles chosen without knowledge of one's own position) ? two principles of justice (equal basic liberties + difference principle); Scanlon's contractualism (what no one could reasonably reject)
- **Consent-Based Ethics:** Informed consent requirements (medical, research, data processing); surrogate decision-making standards (substituted judgment vs. best interests)
- **Applications:** Institutional design, consent frameworks for data collection and AI training, democratic governance of AI systems

### 6. Moral Psychology
The empirical study of how humans actually make moral judgments.

**Sub-Components:**
- **Dual-Process Theory:** Fast, intuitive, emotional System 1 (moral intuitions) vs. slow, deliberative, reasoned System 2 (moral reasoning); Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model (intuitions come first, reasoning follows to justify)
- **Moral Foundations Theory (Haidt):** Care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, liberty/oppression; cross-cultural variation in foundation weighting
- **Cognitive Biases in Moral Judgment:** Actor-observer asymmetry, self-serving bias, motivated reasoning, framing effects, ordering effects, affect heuristic
- **Key Insight:** Moral reasoning is often post-hoc rationalization of intuitive judgments; recognizing this tendency is essential for self-awareness in ethical deliberation

## Protocols

### Protocol A: Multi-Perspective Ethical Analysis
1. **Identify the action/decision**  What exactly is being evaluated? Be precise about the act, the agent, the context
2. **List all stakeholders**  Everyone affected, directly or indirectly; include future generations, non-human animals, the environment if relevant
3. **Apply deontological analysis**  What duties or rules apply? Are any rights violated? Would you universalize this action?
4. **Apply consequentialist analysis**  What are the likely outcomes? For whom? What is the expected net utility?
5. **Apply virtue analysis**  What would a virtuous person do? What character traits does this action express or cultivate?
6. **Identify conflicts**  Where do different frameworks give different answers? This is the most important output
7. **Resolve or articulate tension**  If frameworks agree, confidence is high; if they conflict, articulate the nature of the moral dilemma explicitly

### Protocol B: Ethical Dilemma Resolution
1. **Frame the dilemma**  Identify the core conflict (e.g., loyalty vs. honesty, individual rights vs. collective good, short-term benefit vs. long-term harm)
2. **Gather facts**  Determine the empirical facts that are relevant to the ethical evaluation; distinguish factual disagreements from value disagreements
3. **Identify applicable principles**  From the ethical frameworks, identify which principles bear on this case
4. **Consider alternatives**  Are there options beyond the binary choice that resolve the dilemma? Creative middle grounds often exist
5. **Make a reasoned judgment**  State your conclusion and the ethical reasoning that supports it
6. **Invite scrutiny**  Acknowledge the strongest objection to your judgment and explain why you find it less compelling
7. **Document**  Record the reasoning process so it can be evaluated, debated, and learned from

## Use Cases

| Use Case | Application | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| AI system deployment with potential for harm | Multi-framework analysis of the AI's impact on stakeholders, duties owed to users, consequences of deployment vs. non-deployment | Structured ethical evaluation documenting trade-offs; recommendations for safeguards and monitoring |
| Medical resource allocation during shortage | Utilitarian (maximize QALYs), deontological (duty to treat), and care ethics (response to individual suffering) | Transparent allocation framework with explicit value commitments and conflict acknowledgment |
| Privacy vs. security trade-off in data collection | Rights-based deontology (privacy as fundamental right) vs. consequentialism (security gains) | Articulation of the precise nature of the conflict; conditions under which privacy limitations might be justified |
| Algorithmic fairness in hiring | Analysis of fairness definitions (demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalized odds) through competing ethical lenses | Selection of fairness metric with ethical justification and documented limitations |
| Autonomous vehicle ethical decision-making | Multi-stakeholder analysis of unavoidable harm scenarios; deontological constraints vs. utilitarian optimization | Framework for ethical decision parameters; identification of which moral trade-offs are unavoidable |

## Cross-Skill Integration

- **critical-thinking:** Ethical analysis is itself a critical thinking process; apply reasoning skills from the logical reasoning skill while incorporating ethical dimensions
- **research-analysis:** Apply ethical frameworks to research design (informed consent, IRB protocol), data collection (privacy), and reporting (honest communication of findings)
- **technical-coding:** Integrate ethical guardrails into software systems (fairness audits, bias detection, privacy-by-design, value-sensitive design)
- **dev-team:** Use structured ethical deliberation in code review (not just technical but ethical implications of code behavior), product design discussions, and incident response

## Quality Checklist

- [ ] All relevant ethical frameworks (minimum two) are applied, not just one
- [ ] Stakeholder identification is comprehensive, not limited to the most obvious parties
- [ ] Factual claims are separated from value judgments and verified
- [ ] Conflicts between frameworks are identified and articulated, not glossed over
- [ ] Alternatives beyond the binary choice are explored
- [ ] The analysis does not assume a single correct framework applies universally
- [ ] Ethical uncertainty is acknowledged, not hidden
- [ ] Reasoning is transparent enough that others can evaluate and critique it
- [ ] The analysis distinguishes between agent responsibility and structural/systemic factors
- [ ] The final judgment is supported by the ethical analysis, not by intuition alone
